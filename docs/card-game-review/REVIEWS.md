### Source findings

1. **Magic: The Gathering** — Wizards of the Coast, [How to Play](https://magic.wizards.com/en/how-to-play), sections “Casting Spells,” “Taking a Turn,” and “Combat.” The beginner guide states a land is tapped to generate mana, a player may play one land each turn, and cards untap in the beginning phase. The combat overview orders attacker selection, defender blocker selection, and damage; creatures with damage at least equal to toughness die. Its end-step overview says damage is removed from surviving creatures. The source describes a multi-creature battle, not this project's proposed one-active/reserve layout. It is a beginner summary; detailed exceptions were not checked.

2. **ONE PIECE CARD GAME** — Bandai, [Play Guide](https://en.onepiece-cardgame.com/play-guide/), sections “What You Need,” “Card Types,” “Playmat & Card Zones,” and “Turn Flow.” It describes a separate 10-card DON!! deck, resources acquired automatically each turn (normally two, first player one on first turn), available to pay costs or boost a Leader or Character; rested cards refresh and attached DON!! returns to the cost area during refresh. The guide distinguishes the Leader, up to five Characters and their zones; Characters can attack opposing Leaders or Characters. This is a leader-plus-characters arrangement, **not** an active-fighter/reserve system. The guide is an overview; exceptional interactions were not checked. Its [rules index](https://en.onepiece-cardgame.com/rules/#rulebook) lists an official manual, comprehensive rules and playsheet; their PDF contents were not inspected.

3. **Digimon Card Game** — Bandai, [Let's play the Digimon Card Game!](https://world.digimoncard.com/about/001.php), section “What kind of game is it?” The publisher describes a Digimon in the battle area attacking, evolving, becoming stronger and gaining new power; its high-level win summary involves reducing opposing security cards to zero and delivering a final blow. This introductory page does **not** specify evolution costs, stacking, timing, or inheritance; do not infer those details from it. The [official rule index](https://world.digimoncard.com/rule/) lists a manual and comprehensive rules dated September 18, 2026, but their PDFs were not inspected.

### Proposed design lessons and unverified risks — not sourced rules or approved decisions

- **Active/reserve and combat:** Unlike the comparators above, the human baseline specifically suggests a single active and a reserve. Define who may attack, who can be targeted, how replacement occurs, and what happens with no available replacement. Write the order of damage, destruction, loss triggers, evolution and victory checks; a lethal hit on RAGE and a simultaneous DEATH trigger particularly need explicit handling. A simple ordering and printed reference can reduce disputes. Hypothesis: an unattackable reserve that evolves freely may create a safe snowball; compare active-only vs reserve-eligible progression in later playtests.
- **Resources:** Magic illustrates drawing resource cards and reusing spent sources; ONE PIECE illustrates resources separate from the main deck and automatic acquisition. They are distinct tradeoffs, not evidence that either is better. For the baseline's main-deck resource cards, consider a bounded anti-starvation mechanism and a useful late-game resource outlet. If HOPE gains progress from spending, check that players cannot repeatedly spend/refund the same resource for free growth; define when and how often an expenditure qualifies. Automatic separate supply would be a departure from the illustrative baseline and should be identified if adopted.
- **Evolution and loss:** A growing unit can provide a legible sense of development (Digimon source supports only this high-level observation). Use this project's distinct class-action triggers, not the comparator's specific system; define stage prerequisites, excess progress, whether an event affects multiple eligible fighters, and reset on leaving play. Hypotheses: self-damage farming may make RAGE automatic; token sacrifice loops may make DEATH dominant; LUCK may stagnate if successful hits are inaccessible. Test each alongside HOPE rather than assuming balance.
- **Physical state:** Consider separate distinguishable damage and progression markers and a stage indicator printed on each fighter's reference; avoid one ambiguous pile. Specify whether damage persists between turns and through switching/evolution. Test setup, occupied reserve slots, simultaneous triggers and marker counts with two humans. Alternative: track progression on a shared reference or single die per fighter; it may save components but can be bumped or obscure ownership. These are untested design suggestions.

### Source findings — attributed, bounded

1. **Shared tracks, individual progress, cross-path choice.** Stonemaier Games, designer Jamey Stegmaier, [Tapestry design diary](https://stonemaiergames.com/games/tapestry/design-diary-tapestry/), “August 8, 2019: Advance Turns”: on an advance turn a player chooses one of four tracks, pays a cost, advances their own token one space and gets that space's benefit; a bonus may cost extra. All players have tokens on all four tracks and do not block one another; increasing costs aside, the account says players may focus on one or two tracks. The [product page](https://stonemaiergames.com/games/tapestry/) confirms four tracks and focus-versus-balance choices. These are shared linear tracks, not evidence of a branching fighter-class tech tree, secret progress, unrestricted respec, or deck modifications. The structure is shared while progress is individual, not a common progress pool; tokens on the board are public, not hidden.

2. **Card acquisition and visibly tracking a card's changing state.** The same diary, “August 14: Tech Cards,” says an invent benefit gains a tech card from three face-up options or a random deck and places it in a bottom row. A later upgrade moves a selected owned card to a middle row and provides its printed benefit; a further move to the top row requires a specified prerequisite fulfilled by the player or a neighboring opponent and gives the next benefit. These cards stay in a tableau as described, not shuffled deck cards; the diary does not establish this project's specific upgrade persistence, hidden-zone handling or fighter rules. The opponent-prerequisite option illustrates a way an opponent's progress can help eligibility, but is not a demonstrated anti-snowball outcome.

3. **In-match deck acquisition and delayed access.** Wise Wizard Games, [Star Realms: Learn to Play](https://www.starrealms.com/learn-to-play), “Initial Setup” and “Building Your Deck”: five face-up cards form a shared Trade Row; a gained row card is immediately replaced from the shared deck, and a separate stack offers another purchasable card type. Acquired cards enter the buyer's discard pile; when the personal deck runs out, the discard is shuffled into a new personal deck. The same guide describes persistent bases until destroyed and says combat can destroy enemy bases. This is an introductory overview; exact timing exceptions or the effectiveness of counterplay are not established. Acquisition to discard is deck expansion, not modification of an existing copy; the market is contested, unlike a guaranteed personal pool.

4. **Physical copy identity and irreversible component tradeoff.** Dire Wolf Digital, [Clank! Legacy FAQ](https://www.direwolfdigital.com/clank-legacy-acquisitions-incorporated/faq/), “Upper Management Pack and The C Team Pack,” Contract: where multiple identical cards are in the row, the relevant effect applies to the specific chosen copy; the FAQ suggests marking that copy with a cube and removing it when no longer applicable. Its “General Non-Rules Questions” states the campaign permanently alters components, including stickers, writing and destruction; its “Clarifications with Minor Spoilers,” Stickering Cards, warns sticker overhang may need trimming. This is a legacy campaign and the temporary row marker is not evidence of a persistent hidden-deck upgrade. It demonstrates the physical distinction between copy identification, temporary state, and irreversible modification, not a ready-made reusable solution.

5. **Physical reference and exploit observations.** Stonemaier's diary, “August 21: Rules!”, reports a separate reference guide describing 48 advancement benefits, with two copies, plus player reference cards. “August 16: Debugging Tapestry,” by Morten Monrad Pedersen, reports that limited negative interaction and engine building could enable snowballing; the team found excessive combinations in their own development, adjusted them, and rechecked. This is the authors' account of their own game, not an experiment or evidence of balance in ours. The diary's August 9 entry also reports pieces originally on tracks obscured information from certain angles, prompting a separate board for them.

### Designer synthesis — researcher's provisional suggestions, not approved rules

- **Common tree / independently owned progression:** Use the same public printed node map with separate, distinguishable owner markers or duplicated small player boards; one player's unlock must neither consume the other's node nor activate its effect. Specify individually unlocked nodes rather than only track position if branches can coexist. Define mixed-class eligibility from a stable, checkable class source and say whether losing that source removes eligibility/effects. Printed node IDs plus two concise reference sheets avoid relying on color alone. Tapestry supports separate player tokens on common linear tracks only; the branching/class adaptation is our untested proposal.
- **Adaptation and cost:** Permit early entry into another path while retaining previous unlocks, with costs paid from resources that also fund immediate defense or combat. Illustrative hypothesis: a defending player who sees an opponent's economy engine can spend the next development opportunity on an anti-engine action rather than the next defense node; the sacrifice is a delayed defensive upgrade. Define the new route's prerequisite and earliest actionable turn; if respec is allowed, state whether benefits/cards already gained are removed and how to prevent cycling for repeated rewards. This is not a tested trace or recommendation to copy a game.
- **New cards:** Give each player a finite, enumerated personal unlock pool so the same node remains available to both; choose the new card by node, reveal the acquisition and put it in a specified public discard or private hand, then explicitly define when it can be drawn/played. Discard avoids immediate surprise but may be too slow in a short match; hand gives immediate response but strengthens tempo. Specify exhaustion, quantities, maximum deck size/copy legality and the absence or timing of any shuffle. A growing deck can dilute the chance of drawing a needed resource: test with actual starter deck and match length.
- **Existing-card upgrade:** For reusable physical play, prefer a fully printed replacement card from a finite pool, exchanged for exactly one identifiable eligible copy in a public zone; place the replaced base in an out-of-play holding area. Define whether the upgraded version follows that copy through hand/deck/discard, its identical opaque sleeve/back, reset after the match, copy limits, replacement effects, missing target and shuffle/information exposure. Do not put a loose marker on a card shuffled into a hidden deck; it can detach or mark the deck. A type-wide node modifier on a reference is simpler, but explicitly affects all qualifying copies and is not the same as an instance upgrade. State whether multiple upgrades stack or are mutually exclusive.
- **Anti-snowball is a hypothesis, not a sourced result:** Avoid development points awarded solely for winning combat; consider a fixed, capped per-player schedule or another catch-up-access rule independent of damage dealt, while making upgrades compete with resource expenditure. Test whether an early leader can convert more resources into additional unlocks anyway. Test disruption from behind, effects triggered by repeated sacrifice/refunds, protected reserve farming, and whether the leader also receives the best response. A node granting new income should not automatically finance unlimited new nodes.
- **Bookkeeping checks:** Enumerate every marker, board, replacement and pool copy per player; place persistent effects next to owner rather than underneath fighters. Keep damage, evolution progress and node ownership distinguishable, with no reading blocked by markers; list event order and reset. During actual future physical sessions record time spent finding/swapping cards, missed triggers, ambiguous ownership, accidental hidden information, and component shortage. No physical tests have yet occurred.
